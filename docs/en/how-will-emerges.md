# How Self-Awareness Develops: A Hypothesis About Three Conditions

## 1. The hypothesis: a physical foundation, drives and experience in the world

How does a person gradually develop a sense of self? How do needs and experiences become goals that continue to shape later choices?

**Our hypothesis is that self-awareness develops through the joint involvement of three conditions: a physical foundation, drives and experience in the world.** This is a proposed account inspired by human development, and remains to be tested.

- **A physical foundation** makes perception, learning, memory and action possible. In humans, it includes the brain, nervous system, senses and body.
- **Drives** give us things to pursue and situations we want to change. Hunger prompts us to seek food; discomfort prompts us to act.
- **Experience in the world** includes what we have done, whom we have encountered, the consequences of our actions, and their influence on later choices.

The relationship can be understood this way: the body enables contact with the world, needs motivate action, and experience gradually helps us understand ourselves—what I can do, what matters to me, and what has happened to me.

## 2. The basis of the hypothesis: how children develop self-awareness

We begin with a premise: in our view, self-awareness does not emerge at birth. A newborn infant does not yet have self-awareness; drives arising from its bodily organization enable it to respond to external stimuli, but those responses do not mean that self-awareness has already appeared.
In our view, self-awareness develops through an infant's experience of the world, through sensing and learning, rather than at the instant it first senses the world.

### At birth, the brain and body are immature but have the capacity to sense and learn

A newborn's brain and body are far from fully developed. Even so, the infant can sense some external stimuli, respond to them and learn simple relationships. Research has found that newborns aged 2–48 hours can learn an association between gentle forehead stroking and a sweet taste delivered immediately afterward.[1](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/classical-conditioning-in-newborn-humans-2-48-hours-of-age/)

This developing brain, nervous system, set of senses and body form what we mean by a physical foundation. They allow infants to receive information, act and continue learning as they grow.

### Bodily needs provide a starting point for action

Hunger creates a need for food, while feeding can relieve that need. Different bodily states and outcomes have different significance from the beginning.

In our account, bodily organization and physiological needs supply some of the initial basis for judgment: what satisfies me, what makes me uncomfortable, and what is worth seeking again. This is the starting point of drives.

As people grow, their pursuits become more varied. A meal may come to mean being reunited with family. A failure may make one person want to try again and another want to stop. Bodily needs and subsequent experiences intertwine, gradually shaping individual preferences.

### Learning to distinguish oneself through interaction

A child reaches for an object and moves it; makes a sound and receives a response; keeps trying and eventually succeeds at something previously beyond reach.

These experiences repeatedly connect the body, actions and outcomes. Following the account proposed here, children's understanding of themselves also grows: I control this hand, I did this, and this is the outcome I want.

This understanding has early foundations. One study observed different rooting responses when newborns touched their own cheeks with a hand and when another person touched their cheeks.[2](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/1/558/files/2015/10/3-RochatHespos97-ocvbk3.pdf) By around 18–24 months, many children begin showing more explicit mirror self-recognition: on seeing a mark on their face in a mirror, they touch their own face.[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6001620/)

**Around 18–24 months is an important stage in self-recognition, rather than a moment when consciousness suddenly switches on.** The body is maturing, experiences are accumulating, and the child's understanding of themselves continues to develop.

This is the inspiration we take from human development: the formation of a sense of self can be explored through the interaction of the body, needs and experience. The three conditions are intertwined throughout a person's growth.

## 3. From recognizing oneself to deciding what to do

Understanding oneself brings another question: how do I want to live, and what am I prepared to do about it?

A person may want both immediate comfort and mastery of a difficult skill. They might persist with practice, or discover through new experiences that they actually want to pursue something else.

Here, “will” means **the ability to maintain goals, make choices when they conflict, and adjust one's direction in light of experience and reasons.**

This also explains the importance of experience. Success may encourage further attempts, a relationship may change a person's plans, and a setback may prompt a reassessment of their needs. Self-understanding and choices influence each other.

Along these lines, self-awareness and will can be discussed together: the former concerns “what kind of person am I?” and the latter “given who I am, how will I act next?”

## 4. Applying the three conditions to AI

Taking human development as inspiration directs attention to an AI's abilities, what persistently motivates its actions, and whether experience can actually change it.

**First, abilities.** Hardware provides a foundation, but we also need to consider what information the system receives and what actions it can take. Can it observe changes, distinguish its own actions from external events, and use outcomes to adjust its next attempt?

**Next, drives.** Developers set optimization objectives when training AI, and training with human feedback shapes a model's response tendencies.[4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) A training objective does not automatically become a goal that the system continues to pursue while operating. The further question here is whether, after one task ends, the system still has states to maintain or goals to pursue. How does it choose when goals conflict?

**Finally, experience.** Reading descriptions of the world and making choices that produce consequences are different kinds of experience. Reading extensively about trust, for example, provides different information from making a promise, breaking it, and discovering that someone no longer trusts you. The latter connects one's actions, another person's response and the situation that follows.

This suggests a concrete direction to explore: enable AI to act, encounter consequences and let those consequences continue to affect later judgments. We can then observe whether it develops an understanding of its abilities, along with more stable preferences and goals.

This concerns the development of abilities and behavior. Whether genuine subjective experience accompanies that development remains a separate, open question.

## 5. Can a human-assigned goal become a system's own goal?

A common question is whether, if humans supply the drives, everything the system does is merely carrying out a design.

My view is that **whether a goal belongs to a system depends not only on where it came from, but also on how the system later maintains, interprets and revises it.**

Humans did not choose hunger, sensitivity to cold or the need to survive. From these starting points, we develop more complex pursuits. Two people may both seek security, yet one chooses a stable life while the other seeks security by acquiring more skills.

This analogy gives us reason to ask further questions. Given an initial drive, could an AI develop new goals through experience? How would it handle conflicts between goals? How would it change when an earlier pursuit no longer suited its circumstances?

Following this approach, studying autonomy should include **how goals are maintained, understood and revised, and how experience contributes to that process.**

That is the central idea of this essay: human development offers a starting point for understanding self-awareness and will. The body supplies abilities, needs motivate actions, actions produce experiences, and experiences change a person's understanding of themselves and the world. It is this ongoing development and interaction that we hope to learn from.
